The rail demarcates the pine grove steeped in haze and the torpid lake which stretches unto the base of a mountain barely visible beyond the hazy winter, the apparition of a white leviathan. Flanked by uncertain views, from above, the rail’s interjacent nature seemingly slices the scenery into two distinct halves: wood and water, as if they could not possibly coexist, much less come to understand one another. Likewise, it has estranged—perhaps they had always been estranged—two seemly individuals sitting on opposite sides of the empty train car: a girl near the lake and a boy near the woods.

They have names but we will not use them.

The girl is unremarkable: short hair, pale blouse, wool cardigan, slacks; she’s entirely innocuous, to the extent that one might even find her sterile. In fact, even her face is best described as uniquely average. Astonishingly so. The boy across stares at her not because he finds her particularly attractive, or because she’s leaning out of the window, cigarette in hand, trying to blow the smoke outside in vain, some of which is inevitably blown into the car, but because of her dull face.

It occurs to us that perhaps the boy is jumping hastily to a conclusion and therefore has missed some crucial detail. He wonders why one of the buttons from her cardigan is missing, wonders why she smokes Newports, wonders why she’s on the train; wonders because it is all that he can possibly wonder, but in the end, his speculations are in vain; her unyielding countenance is plastered in monotonous veneer.

So the boy patiently awaits, musing upon trivial matters to kill time when they arrive at a stop (not his) and the girl rises, leaving a wrinkled letter, which presumably has been in her possession for a long time given the condition of the envelope. The girl takes notice of her abandoned note, briefly looking back, a fleeting reflexive dart of the eyes, but continues towards the exit of the train, ultimately disembarking. By the time the boy notices this, retrieves the letter, and promptly flits to the exit, the doors have already closed, leaving the two figures separated once again, this time by a physical barrier. Watching the girl walk past the platform, through the ticket gate, the boy attempts to draw her attention by spreading his arms wide and waving in vain; and when she turns the corner he catches a glimpse of her blurry profile obscured by exhaled smoke.
***
By the time the train started along once again, I had given up. If it were her desire to leave it behind, be it so. One might even suspect that she left it intentionally. Given how much time I have left until my destination, I may as well inspect the contents of the envelope.

The envelope was severely tattered, certain corners torn and damp, as if she clutched the note in her sleep, refusing to ever let go, an odd contradiction to the current turn of events. Indeed, the exterior of the note was so weathered that it took on the texture of bathroom tissue. The contents of the envelope revealed a letter, meticulously written in tired, tremulous words, this too, torn.
***
Darling,

It’s been quite some time since I wrote to you, I know, which may come across as cruel considering I’ve received all your letters, but please know that I’m writing this letter with the utmost haste. You must feel abandoned, but I beg of you, understand that this is not the case in the least. I’ve been through some difficult times as well, and will proceed to explain myself thoroughly, as best I can.

Since I wrote, I’ve been transferred to another facility, a promotion so to speak, and am writing from a small wooden desk positioned against a window with a view of a certain lake (as usual, you know I cannot reveal too much of my location; they don’t allow that). It’s rather peaceful here and I am sure you’d like it too. The tranquility of this place is partly owed to its secluded location, but also to the peculiar predicament that I am the only occupant of the low-rise apartment complex. No one else lives here. But it never gets lonely—not in the conventional sense at least—since I have my subjects to read.

The facility is some miles north of the establishment. Though it is hardly a distance that warrants a vehicle, a chauffeur collects me in the morning and returns me in the evening. I judged him to be a middle-aged man, though I cannot be certain due to the mask obscuring his face at all times. Even his hands are covered by impenetrable black leather gloves. Through the slits in his mask, I can barely discern the brown of his eyes. Once, I asked for his name. He said he didn’t have one, so I started calling him “Name” as a slight jest during one of our first meetings, but he didn’t seem to object, and it has stuck ever since.

A severely reticent man, Name relates everything by means of gestures. Since my relocation, I’ve learnt to discern the myriad gestures employed, distinguished by unremarkable differences. A slight bow of roughly forty degrees communicates what I believe to be gratitude, whilst the same bow, yet infinitesimally lower by around five degrees, relates acknowledgment. These are among the panoply of symbols that have confounded me. Depending on the context, one may be mired in many possible interpretations of a single gesture, and it has troubled me greatly over the last months as to what Name intends to relate.

Once, late in the evening, while we were motoring along the groves, a ray of dying light seeped through the slit in his mask, and from his reflection in the mirror, I realized that his eyes were in fact blue, not brown: a hysterical blue of a tumultuous sea.

In any case, after Name leaves me at the facility followed by a prompt bow, I change into a rubber suit in the changing room and enter the main room, part of the edifice lacking at my previous post (the last facility had cubicles). The main room has no windows or lighting; it has a high ceiling and is perfectly cuboid; the ceiling and floor appear to be constructed of the same material (plain white linoleum?), though this is uncertain on account of the room being pitch black, save the two illuminated path markings converging at the depression in the center of the room which girdles a bed. This arrangement seems to infinitely extend in both directions, with each unit of repetition containing its own bed. One would never suspect such a stately chamber from the facade.

Unlike my previous post where I was made to read the subjects through wires and electrodes—not unlike those cheap science fiction stories—the new suit provided by the department allows me to do the same by simply wrapping my arms around the subject in a special bed. I presume you would find this practice somewhat uncomfortable, perhaps you might even feel envious, but I assure you, the ritual is nothing like being with you, is without a trace of affection, and is only performed out of necessity to make the books. Tormenting you could not be further from my intentions.

As you know, while I’m reading and transcribing, a process that can take days, surrogates are dispatched to replace the subjects. It's disheartening that those closest to the subjects appear to be oblivious to the drastic change, and over time, one cannot help but feel pity for them. Oh, how replaceable people are! Even the most incompetent surrogates are able to see the job through. In the entire history of the ministry, only once or twice have the surrogates been found out.

The actual process of reading entails, as I say, embracing the unconscious subject at the center of the room upon the bed, which is far less a physical phenomenon than an incorporeal inhabitation of the subject, akin to fitting a hand into a rubber glove and pressing each finger to the very tips, ensuring a perfect fit. This took some cautious practice at first, as one can find a rather treacherous world within a subject.

Reality permits an event to happen only once; the mind allows it to unfold endlessly. As such, certain memories can expand with time and swell infinitely; they are small worlds unto themselves. One must avoid such quagmires when reading by taking the proper precautions. A competent reader must wade through clouds of distractions for transcriptions sent to the library of names.

When I began, I requested access to the library—for reference, as I was still new to the job, but I was promptly denied. I was never told what they used the books for or why the library existed. I was merely told to do my job and complied. They have yet to allow me access to the library of names.

You may wonder whether all this reading takes a toll on the mind, and yes, it does. This was part of the reason for the hiatus in our correspondence—
***
At this point, while writing the letter, the girl looks up from her desk and stares emptily out of the window. We can see the gelatinous glimmer of her eyes, through which she is neither seeing the lake nor the distant mountain, or anything, really. Her eyes simply flit without agency. In fact, she believes these images unnecessary and that she would be perfectly content without them.

The morning fog dissipates out of the white valley, crawling over the crests, this too, a mere reflection drifting across her eyes. She resolves to take a walk.

She was fraying like string, somehow increasingly porous, her boundaries overwhelmingly malleable, and detected this in her trembling fingers and her empty yet quivering eyes.

Exiting her dwelling, down the external staircase, she made her way towards the small path winding through the groves. Her apprehension (?) was partly caused by an incident involving a minor mix-up of subjects that occurred approximately a fortnight ago; she was assigned another reader, which is usually prohibited on account of the sheer volume of essences exchanged. And now, she was subject to the turbulent torments of identity.
***
I must say, most subjects are fairly easy to read: the unprovoked, peevish brute; the selfish, self-pitying soul; the philistine imp; malleable as they are, I have no particular issue with these subjects, unlike most readers, as they make my job easier. They’ve ordered me to transcribe, but I’m not too fond of the term myself, and fancy thinking of the process as akin to *grabbing handles*. Most individuals are easy to get a grip on, and therefore easy to read.

Not much stands between me and these handles. Like the hag I read the other day. Pitifully simple. Within her modest world, a handful of compliments and questions were all it took for me to grasp her—stroke her phallus as it were. No wading through memories was required on my part; nor did I encounter the labyrinth of contradictions, of discord present in a mere handful of subjects. As I recall, only once did I lose myself within such a disquieting predicament.

He was a gangly, ungainly individual of tall stature, pasty complexion, though this was unfortunately a latent discovery. I inferred that this might have been for two potential reasons: the simple ineptitude of my undiscerning eye (which I find quite improbable); or the subconscious erasure so to speak, obvious in his ebbing presence. As such—he appeared upon first impression, despite his height—a small, innocuous man; an easy subject, I thought at first and I hoped for a much briefer reading. Alas, this was not so.

I pushed him into my chest upon the bed.

A veiled Möbius strip (in all honesty, I was not certain of the mass having any defined conformation as it was covered in some sort of hazy, shapeless matter) floated at the center of the patchwork realm. Its seams were only loosely drawn, and if provided the right opportunity, one could tear their way through to enter another realm; and yet another realm, infinitely extending in all possible directions. Here, there was no conception of color. Though the rooms appeared uniform, each space was slightly altered, as I observed the stitches in differing arrangements, yet the seemingly identical object remained suspended in the center—rather, this was the impression I was fed. There were no certainties in the realm. This too was not a preexisting conception.

As I approached what I surmised to be the object, I found its bounds fading, dissolving, silently dissipating into nothingness, so as to give the impression of a mirage, after which another seemingly identical object would draw my attention in the periphery of my vision—this too, as I came to find, another illusion. I have since questioned this odd phenomenon, for these objects seemed to be placed intentionally, as if to misdirect, but I was rather vexed at the time, and lacked the composure for such trivial musings. With each illusory object, by degrees, irritation became aggravation, aggravation became fury, and ultimately I found myself utterly livid (as you may find yourself in my circumstance), as the pathetic man had no business being so elusive, adding unnecessary inconvenience to the lengthy list of subjects awaiting me for transcription; so that by the time I finally caught the object, this time, a tangible substance, I was hasty and justifiably violent in detaining the abhorrent thing.

It writhed between my fingers, countless legs twisting and scraping like a centipede seized in a futile struggle for freedom. But my grip was practiced, and it would not be allowed to escape. Not even the most elusive individual can escape me, if ever there was one.

Now it screamed its ghastly screech, an abomination which reverberated through the cranium, and I found it immediately necessary to quell its vile manner. By then, I was quite practiced, a swift motion of the instrument, a sharp glint, though the ministry officially disapproved of this and took a dim view of such methods. In any case, so long as they did not find out, it was of no concern to me. It made my job easier as I was able to observe what lies beneath; and besides, everything was fine so long as I stitched them back up in the end. And supposing the cut went too deep, irreparably wounding the subject and allowing the damage to manifest upon their return, it was not as though anyone ever noticed.
***
Small branches were scattered across the path returning to the abode, crunching under her feet like countless limbs of countless insects. Trying her best not to snap them, she stole her way down the path. She felt grimy (from the dirt she presumably accumulated on the walk), and therefore resolved to take a long bath. A very long bath.

Perhaps afterwards, she would feel better, the black necrotic knot spasming within her would dissolve in the water, and she would finish writing.

Upon returning to the flat and taking her bath (during which she scrubbed herself intently), she promptly sat before her desk, erasing the last couple of lines. After sitting in silence for some time, she took up her pen.
***
I am sure, provided your character, that you would even be curious about the mundane details of my daily existence, so I feel I should go on to explain, beginning with the apartment complex. The complex consists of two stories, but only the first is visible from the exterior; the second is hidden behind the large sloping roof that takes up at least half the facade, not unlike those traditional Japanese straw thatched houses. Of course, the roof is constructed of some other material, but the silhouette is rather similar.

Within, when upturned upon the bed, the constricted skylight reveals the narrow sky, which, on a quiet night, encircles the snowy moon. I am barely visible in the light. In the dimness, its pale light filters through, presently obscured by the collecting snow upon the roof; and as the darkness deepens, crawling up the bed towards my figure, I am ultimately left in the silence of the night, my only respite the hope that I should fall asleep and be transported to the morning. When it finally arrives, the colorless rays crawl through the seams in the snow, reaching my eyes as I wake; and yet the warmth that I yearn for is absent—at least nothing palpable. And it has traveled so far to meet me, only to be hindered by the narrow skylight; why should it exert itself any further and deliver warmth? I could venture outside and meet it myself, but some indefatigable force imprisons me within. Should I have the strength, the insanity to tear myself out, I am certain I would dissolve much like the snow.

But I digress. Perhaps I should also describe a particular subject as a means of impressing upon you what daily life entails.

As I said, reading is arduous work, but rewarding nonetheless. It was a typical day: I was received at the facility after being delivered by Name, and was waiting upon the bed for the subject to be brought in.

The subject was a gangly, ungainly individual of tall stature, pasty complexion, though this was difficult to discern for one reason or another, most likely due to how dark the facility is. He was a difficult subject, and I may have been a little vexed, but everything went rather peacefully. I will not go into the details, as I am afraid it would bore you, but in any case, it was quite rewarding, and I was able to transcribe successfully, the subject being sent home as he came.

But enough about all that; I am sure you know more than enough by now, and am satisfied with what has been said. I would like to speak of the past.

So, though I digress further, I hope you will countenance discussion of a dinner party which took place before I became a reader. I believe you were also there. Whose party it was I cannot recall (it hardly matters anymore).

It was a night like all the others, the stars above us being homogeneous, as if holes punched into the sky using the same needle.

Amongst the hordes of flesh, you stood, participating in some generic discourse, nodding and smiling as you do. From afar, through fleeting clearings in the crowd, I would chance upon glimpses of your  expression—or rather, my eyes would wind their way through the swaths of heads to seek your unfamiliar face, contorted: a kaleidoscope of expression. I enjoy observing you from afar, knowing that at the end of the night, you would find me too and take my hand, smiling, once again someone familiar.

But when in bed, as you push me into your chest and wrap your arms around me, I feel the darkness enclose me and the moonlight upon my face. My eyes quiver and I feel a gash in my chest, as if someone has taken a blade to it.
***
After musing, she erases the last passage and continues.
***
I realize the letter has gone on far too long, and I am duly sorry for having taken up much of your time.

I hope to hear from you soon and learn of all your doings.
***
When the boy had finished reading the letter, he slipped it carefully into his coat pocket lest it crease. Of course, for a moment, he wondered whether it would do any good to return the letter to its rightful owner, but in the end, he knew that it would fail to make any meaningful difference.

From his seat, the boy had a view of the lake. He promptly made his way to the other side of the car, sitting in the same spot as the girl; and there he remained until his stop, reluctantly alighting from the empty train as another individual boarded.
***
As I (the storyteller often refers to himself as "I") sat at the northern end of the car, I noticed, impressed upon the red velvet seats, traces of previous presence: two imprints counterposed. Speculating about this odd arrangement (derived from mere imprints, this was all that I could do) has led me to draw upon my imagination to contrive this story.

As such, I have no concrete evidence for any of the aforementioned, the only information given to me being a mere vestige.

The train trundled onwards.

After some time, a girl with long blonde hair boarded the train, sitting opposite of me. Her eyes—or more precisely, her pupils—were dilated beyond what I was normally used to (some affliction?), though everything else about her appearance seemed perfectly familiar. Quite satisfied with my classifications, I proceeded to read the cheap paperback I had purchased from a secondhand bookstore: fiction, much like this story, to the very last line.
